Technical sales

How I'd sell GDSFactory+

For years I've been on the builder's side: modeling devices, sketching process stacks, debugging hardware. The part I keep coming back to is the moment a hard idea clicks for someone else, whether it's a TEDx audience, a class of rocketry students or a venue partner for my ballet company.

In EDA sales, explaining the physics is maybe a third of the job. Teams don't buy GDSFactory+ because a ring resonator looks good. They buy it because jumping between Lumerical, KLayout, Virtuoso and home-made Python scripts is delaying tape-outs, burying engineers in DRC fixes and risking six-figure respins. My job is to find that pain, prove the fix on their own design, and make it easy for everyone in the room to say yes.

1 · Discovery before pitching

Ask about the pain, not the product

  • “How many engineer-hours a week go into fixing DRC errors by hand before a tape-out?”
  • “When your foundry updates a PDK layer, who updates your scripts, and how long does it take?”
  • “How many tools does a design pass through between schematic and GDS?”
  • “What did your last respin cost, in money and in schedule?”

2 · The commercial “why”

“We already use open-source gdsfactory. Why pay?”

  • Maintained foundry PDKs. 50+ PDKs kept current by GDSFactory, so the team stops maintaining fragile wrappers of its own.
  • Simulation and verification in one place. Device, circuit and RF simulation plus DRC and LVS in the same environment, instead of hand-offs between tools.
  • Schematic-driven layout and shared workspaces. Code-first engineers and GUI-first designers work on the same design, with an AI agent alongside.
  • Support that ships. PDK updates, training and migration help, so the open-source power users get their time back.

projects/photonic-demo

A ring resonator and an MZI, built in gdsfactory

Two classic silicon-photonics test structures, from Python to GDS to spectrum, in one short script.

I laid out an all-pass ring resonator (10 µm radius, 200 nm gap) and an unbalanced Mach-Zehnder interferometer (40 µm path difference) with gdsfactory's component library. The script writes both to GDSII and renders the layouts. It then models each device's transmission for a 500 × 220 nm silicon strip waveguide at 1550 nm.

Ring FSR

8.1nm

Ring loaded Q

1.6×10⁵

MZI FSR

14.4nm

The FSR follows λ²/(ngL), so a designer can trade footprint against channel spacing in a few lines of code. To check the formula against the real layout, I also simulated the MZI with sax, using the circuit netlist gdsfactory extracts from the layout itself: every bend, straight and splitter as placed. The simulated FSR is 14.36 nm against 14.37 nm from the formula.

projects/qubit-readout-demo

Frequency-multiplexed qubit readout on the quantum PDK

The microwave version of a WDM demux: four readout resonators sharing one line, each tuned to its own frequency.

Superconducting qubits are read out through resonators, each coupled to a shared probe line at its own frequency. I used GDSFactory's superconducting PDK to lay out a transmon with its quarter-wave readout resonator and probe line. Then I used the PDK's simulation models to solve for the resonator length that lands each channel on 6.8, 7.0, 7.2 and 7.4 GHz.

TargetResonator lengthSimulated f₀LinewidthLoaded Q
6.8 GHz4,462 µm6.7999 GHz111 kHz61,500
7.0 GHz4,334 µm6.9999 GHz120 kHz58,500
7.2 GHz4,214 µm7.2000 GHz129 kHz55,800
7.4 GHz4,099 µm7.3999 GHz139 kHz53,200

Higher frequencies need shorter resonators, about 120 µm less per 200 MHz step. Channels 200 MHz apart with 0.1 MHz linewidths leave plenty of room, so many qubits can share one readout line.

Coupled quantum dots as neural electric-field sensors

Can a pair of InAs quantum dots, read out optically, feel the field of a single firing neuron without touching it?

In a stacked pair of quantum dots, an electron and hole can sit in neighboring dots and form an indirect exciton. That state carries a permanent dipole, so its photoluminescence line shifts linearly with any applied field (the quantum-confined Stark effect). I modeled the sensor as a two-level system and the neuron as a line of charge, then compared the two.

ĥ = [ εh −t ; −t εh + F·d ] · two-level Hamiltonian
p = q·d = 10 e·nm · ≈ 1 meV of shift per kV/cm
E(r) = λ / (2π ε₀ r) · neuron as a line charge

To model the real devices, I used the group's Mathematica notebooks for coupled quantum-dot molecules. I diagonalized the Hamiltonians for two dot molecules (tunnel couplings of 1 meV and 500 µeV) to map their photoluminescence energies against applied field, and plotted the dipole fields of the electron-hole configurations.

Neuron field at 4 mm

2.2×10⁻³kV/cm

CQD resolution

1.7×10⁻³kV/cm

Detectable to

≈ 5.3mm

This is a first-order bound. Tissue screening, skull attenuation and spectral diffusion would all shrink the real margin, so the next step is a volume-conductor model paired with measured linewidths.
